I'm a social media strategist and content marketer in San Antonio, six-plus years in, most recently running PR and social for the San Antonio Zoo — a regional brand competing for attention against national names with no paid budget behind organic. Along the way: 6M+ audience footprint, 470M+ organic impressions, 15M+ interactions, $75K+ in social-attributed revenue.

This role is different from most social jobs in one way that matters to me: it's not just posting, it's owning — the calendar, the reporting, the agency relationship, and a direct report. I've done all four. I've built content calendars for brands with real stakes, tied social to revenue leadership could defend, directed outside partners, and mentored junior marketers into better ones. That last part is why I'm not treating "manage a direct report" as fine print.

I also want to be straight about the starting point: Chili's social is already good. The Triple Dipper cheese pull was a genuine cultural moment, not a fluke. So I'm not applying to fix a broken feed — I'm applying to own the machine that's working and scale it: keep the calendar full, the trends caught early, the community answered at all hours, and the numbers legible to finance and insights.

One more honest note, in the spirit of "make people feel special": I build my own workflow tools when the software doesn't exist yet — Buffer's product team profiled two of the apps I built on their API. For a Senior Manager role, that's the tell that matters: when a process is slowing the team down, my instinct is to fix the system, not absorb the hours.

The throughline: I like the fun part of social. I also like the spreadsheet, the postmortem, the moderation edge case, the coaching conversation, and the system that makes the fun part repeatable.

The difference between a campaign and a content idea is repeatability. Each of these can create dozens of executions without the core thought getting tired after launch day.

01
Brand platform

Make It Worth Going Out

Chili's owns the feeling behind the value conversation: dinner should be worth the money, the pants, and leaving the couch. Receipt math, delivery absurdities, social observations, OOH-ready copy, and creator stories all ladder to one idea instead of turning the account into a discount feed.

Primary pillar: Worth Going Out For · expandable across organic, paid, creators, OOH, email, and community

Why this matters for Chili's: gives the value conversation an emotional idea bigger than price cuts and keeps the platform useful even when promotions change.

02
Draft + bracket

The Triple Dipper Draft

Treat Triple Dipper combinations like fantasy football. Creators draft their three, Chili's publishes picks and power rankings, fans argue over reaches and steals, and the winning build gets crowned. The product naturally creates the mechanic.

Primary pillar: Cravings & Chaos · ideal March tentpole, reusable on menu drops

Why this matters for Chili's: turns an already-social product into a participatory event with creator, community, and repeatable seasonal legs.

03
Recurring show

Booth Confessions

Turn a Chili's booth into a recognizable social set. Couples, friends, employees, creators, and fans answer questions, admit petty crimes against shared appetizers, or submit anonymous confessions. Chili's becomes the host of the conversations people already have there.

Primary pillar: Booth Behavior · episodic short-form + Stories + community submissions

Why this matters for Chili's: creates a recognizable show format that owns the human behavior happening inside the restaurant, not just the food on the table.

04
Fandom series

The Chili's Lore Department

Archive content treated with absurd seriousness: old menus, commercials, discontinued items, restaurant photos, fan artifacts, and brand history. Let the community submit evidence, investigate it, and occasionally bring something back because social found it.

Primary pillar: Chili's Lore · built for nostalgia, comments, search, and fan participation

Why this matters for Chili's: converts decades of brand equity into searchable, shareable fandom instead of leaving the archive dormant.

05
Creator universe

The ChiliHeads

Build a recurring cast instead of buying one-off posts. A Margarita Correspondent. Commissioner of Triple Dippers. Director of Chili's Lore. Employees and creators return often enough that fans recognize them — turning the account into programming with characters.

Primary pillar: ChiliHeads · creator, employee, community, and recruiting engine

Why this matters for Chili's: gives creators, employees, and regulars recurring roles so the audience builds familiarity with people as well as products.

Straight from the job description, here's each core responsibility and how I'd actually run it.

What the first 90 days look like

Days 1–30

Learn the machine

  • Audit channel performance, content mix, and recurring formats.
  • Map approvals, agency responsibilities, moderation, and reporting.
  • Meet the direct report, Creative, Insights, Finance, and key operators.
  • Identify what already works well enough to protect.
Days 31–60

Sharpen the system

  • Refine the content pillars against real performance data.
  • Lock a practical voice + escalation playbook with the agency.
  • Build a reporting view that connects social behavior to business outcomes.
  • Give the direct report clear ownership of meaningful work.
Days 61–90

Ship + prove

  • Launch or test one repeatable tentpole from the campaign bank.
  • Establish a trend-response rhythm that can move inside 24 hours.
  • Review what changed, what moved, and what gets scaled next.
  • Leave the team with a clearer calendar and fewer invisible handoffs.

The operating responsibilities

  1. Own the organic calendar — what, when, and why. One board where every post has an owner, an asset, a status, and a reason it exists. The pillars keep it full; the campaigns give it tentpoles; every slot still has to earn its place by entertaining, informing, or educating the audience — with the business outcome attached, not substituted for the audience value.
  2. Own content concept to publish, with the direct report and agencies. Creative gives input on concepts; I drive execution. That means clear briefs, a publishing checklist so nothing goes out with the wrong link, and the direct report owning real pieces, not just chasing assets.
  3. Own reporting end to end. Performance data and social listening in one story that shows how social drives business results — tracked in real time and reconciled with finance and insights, so the numbers match the ones leadership already trusts.
  4. Track culture and trends daily. A standing daily scan with a simple filter: can we say something true, can we ship it in 24 hours, would a guest care. Flag the moment early, while it still matters — the cheese pull worked because someone was watching.
  5. Own the moderation strategy. A written voice, response, and escalation playbook the agency runs to — plus real daily interaction so fans feel heard at all hours. Recurring questions and objections get logged and shipped to Creative and Insights weekly.
  6. Coach and develop the direct report. Clear goals, regular feedback, and a real career path for a junior team member — the part of this role I'm most ready for, and the one most social applicants skip past.

Coaching juniors

  • At Codeup, I mentored junior marketers — the kind of hands-on skill-building and feedback this direct report would get.

Directing the agency

  • Codeup — paid social. Worked with outside paid-social partners around campaign needs, creative inputs, targeting context, and performance so the agency work supported the larger marketing plan.
  • Overland Partners — PR. Directed external PR support around messaging, earned-media opportunities, and the internal context partners needed to represent the brand well.
  • San Antonio Zoo — paid media. Worked with outside paid-ad partners while owning organic social and PR, keeping paid, organic, and campaign priorities from becoming separate conversations.
  • My operating rule: clear briefs, clear owners, fast feedback, and enough context for the agency to be a strategic partner — not a ticket-taking vendor.
